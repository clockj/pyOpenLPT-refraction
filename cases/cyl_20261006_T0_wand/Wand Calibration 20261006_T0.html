The recording

1,519 frames show the whole wand in both cameras

The wand hangs on a string and swings through the cameras' small views (about 26 × 21 mm and 29 × 23 mm at the wand). Most frames lose a ball off the edge, or see the wand end-on so that its balls overlap. The cameras are synchronised and expose for 100 µs, so a ball smears by only about 0.2 px.

Finding the balls

The rod opening separates the balls; no rod mask fits this wand

The detector (the GUI's “ellipse” mode) thresholds the dark wand, removes the rod with an opening, then fits an ellipse to edge points along 96 rays from each ball's centre, leaving out the points in a strip along the rod. This rod is about 75 % of the small ball's height in the image.

Measured rod and blur

The rod is 84–105 px wide in cam1 and 76–96 px in cam2 at the detector's threshold (about 2.2 mm; you measured 2 mm), and wider when it is steep because the tube stretches the image 1.3× across its axis. The edges are blurred by σ ≈ 5–6 px. The usual rule, half-width plus 2σ, asks for a 56 px mask in cam1 and 50 px in cam2, up to 63–73 px for steep rods.

The calibration

Two cameras 61° apart, about 1 m from the tube

Seen along the tube's axis. The fit puts cam1 1,010 mm and cam2 1,042 mm from the axis. Those two distances are exactly what this recording cannot pin down (next section).

Mean residual in each image cell with 20 or more detections, the arrow drawn 500× (0.1 px = 50 px). The pattern changes from cell to cell rather than smoothly, so it is not a lens or tube error.

The main finding

The recording does not fix f: each part finds its own f with the distance in proportion

Long lenses looking at a 30 mm volume from 1 m see almost no perspective: a camera farther away with a longer lens makes nearly the same image. Only the ±1.5 % change of magnification across the volume and the tube's refraction separate the two, and detection errors of a few tenths of a pixel swamp both.

What still holds

Inside the wand's volume the cameras agree

Each variant's cameras triangulate every kept frame, including the frames they were not fitted on. Despite f values from 30,000 to 41,000 px, the wand lengths they measure agree to about 0.1 % on average.

What to change

  1. A wand with a thinner rod: about 1 mm or less for 3–4 mm balls (at most a third of the small ball). The detector was built for rods about a quarter of the ball. Larger balls on the same rod also help.
  2. An independent f for each camera, from the dot board through the tube as on 30 Sep, held fixed in the wand calibration so that the wand only has to find the poses. The calibration cannot hold f yet; it is a small library option. A third camera would also help.
  3. Move the wand through all the depth the cameras keep sharp, and across the whole image. The change of magnification with depth is what separates f from distance.
  4. Keep the whole wand in both views and never point it at a camera. Only 14 % of these frames could be used.
  5. Check the camera mounts. Each camera's image jitters by 0.2–0.3 px horizontally from frame to frame on its own. The calibration absorbs it, but tracked positions will carry it.
