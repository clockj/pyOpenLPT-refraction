How the wand calibration sees through a wall or a tube

pyOpenLPT can calibrate cameras that look into water through a flat wall (the PINPLATE camera model) or through a water-filled tube (PINCYL) from a wand alone: two balls a known distance apart, waved through the volume. Nothing about the cameras is entered, not where they are, where they point or their focal lengths. This page follows both calibrations from the ball detections to the cameras, stage by stage. The flat-wall examples come from the real tank run 20260727/T0; the tube examples are a synthetic three-camera rig around the 20260930 tube.

What goes in

The centres of the two balls in every camera and frame, the wand's length, each image's size, and the window: for flat walls the plate's thickness and refractive indices and which camera looks through which wall; for a tube its radii and indices.

What comes out

Each camera's position, orientation, focal length and lens distortion (k1); for flat walls also each wall's position and angle; error bars; pyOpenLPT camera files (PINPLATE or PINCYL) and a report.

How

A rough start by linear algebra, then a refractive start that is different for walls and tubes, then one least-squares fit of every detection in pixels.

The pipeline

Both calibrations run the same six stages with the same machinery. They differ where the window enters: how the start places the cameras behind it (stage 2), and a few safeguards in the final fit (stage 4).

The two rigs in 3-D

The two examples used throughout the page, as calibrated. Tube: the 20260930 tube, 201.6 mm across with a 3.2 mm acrylic wall, and three copies of its camera 45° apart, 1.09 m from the axis, with a synthetic wand cloud of ±20 mm about the axis.

What a wall and a tube do to an image

Both windows bend every ray, but in different ways, and that difference shapes everything the two calibrations do differently.

A flat wall shifts and slightly stretches the picture, but a ball still looks round: at most 2.4 % longer one way on T0 (test D4). A camera behind it acts almost exactly like a pinhole camera placed farther back with a focal length 1.333× longer, the water's index. The wall's own position barely shows. For a camera looking straight through, moving the wall 10 mm, with the wand moved 3.3 mm to compensate, changes the image by at most px, and only px of that cannot be absorbed by the camera's other parameters. Only oblique cameras see it, and little: px for C3 at °. That is why a wall's position is weakly fixed, the root of most trouble on T0.

A tube bends rays only across its axis, so a ball looks like an ellipse, wider than tall. The stretch depends on where the ball is: for a 2 mm ball it runs from on the far side of the axis to on the near side. That dependence tells the calibration where the tube sits relative to the cameras, to about 13 µm at 0.1 px of noise in the tests (tests/cylinder_wand). The tube's radii are known, so nothing about it needs fitting. The shape does not move a ball's centre (at most px), but a circle no longer fits its outline: use an ellipse detector.

Stage 0both

Data

A ball seen by only one camera cannot be placed in 3-D, so a frame is kept only when each of its two balls is seen by at least two cameras. Each kept detection is one row of the data: frame, camera, ball (A or B), pixel position. On T0 every one of the frames qualifies, detections in all. The automatic start of stage 1 also needs at least 12 frames in which every camera sees both balls.

Stage 1both

An affine start, by linear algebra

Nothing is known about the cameras, so the start has to come from the detections alone, without a guess that could lead a search astray. Stage 1 gets it by pretending every camera is an affine camera, a parallel projection with scaling:

u = Mc X + tc, a 2 × 3 matrix and a shift per camera.

A real camera divides by depth, but when the wand's cloud is shallow compared with the camera's distance that division hardly changes across it. On T0 the cloud's RMS radius is mm and the cameras are about a metre away. A wall's or a tube's refraction over so small a cloud is nearly linear too, and goes into Mc.

  1. One matrix of everything. The detections of the frames that every camera sees go into one matrix: two rows (u, v) per camera, two columns (balls A and B) per frame. Subtracting each row's mean removes every shift tc, because a linear camera maps the cloud's centroid to the detections' centroid.
  2. It has rank 3. What is left is a product, W = M S: M stacks the cameras' 2 × 3 matrices and S holds every ball's x, y, z. A product through a middle dimension of 3 has rank at most 3, so the singular value decomposition's three largest terms give M and S at once (Tomasi–Kanade factorisation). Frames that do not fit, with a wrong detection or swapped balls, are dropped and the decomposition redone.
  3. The wand makes it metric. The factorisation is fixed only up to an unknown 3 × 3 matrix A, M S = (M A⁻¹)(A S): the cloud could be stretched or sheared and the cameras would compensate. Every frame's segment d between its two balls must be one wand length long, dᵀ Q d = L² with Q = AᵀA. That is one linear equation per frame in Q's six entries; least squares over all frames gives Q, and A = Q1/2. It assumes nothing about the cameras, so it also works when a tube makes a camera magnify differently in two directions. The wand must point in many directions for this.
  4. A mirror image stays open. Q1/2 fixes A only up to a rotation, which merely chooses the world's axes, or a reflection. In parallel projection a scene and its mirror image give exactly the same pictures: you cannot tell which end of a wand is nearer. Both go on to stage 2, where perspective decides.

On T0 the step takes s. of the frames are kept, a matrix of × . The fourth singular value is % of the third. What a linear camera cannot describe leaves px per detection, against px for the final calibration, and the wand lengths after the metric step scatter by about %. Even so the start is close:

Stage 2flat wallstube

From parallel projection to the window

Stage 2 turns each linear camera into a real camera behind its window. Each linear camera already gives an orientation, its line of sight and its magnification. The camera's distance along that line is missing, and so is where the window is. The two calibrations find them differently.

flat walls  Pinholes first, then the walls

  1. Distance from perspective. A wand nearer the camera looks longer: its image length per millimetre across the view is f / (D + z) for a wand at depth z beyond the cloud's centre. The ratio of a wand's true length across the view to its image length is therefore a straight line in z, with slope 1/f and intercept D/f (Figure 5, left). Only the right mirror image gives a positive slope; on T0 all four cameras agree on it.
  2. A pinhole fit. Behind a flat wall a camera is almost a pinhole camera in water, with a focal length 1.333× its real one. A pinhole bundle adjustment of all four cameras leaves px on T0, the refraction's misfit, and its focal lengths come out within % of the delivered ones × 1.333.
  3. The walls. For each wall, 92 candidate normals on a cap of 50° about its cameras' mean viewing direction, 9° apart, each at 3 positions between the cameras and the cloud: 276 candidates. For each, the cameras are moved behind it by the paraxial pinhole-to-wall equivalence and their poses fitted against the cloud (small_angle_camera, resect). The best 8 are refined with the wall free, and the best 3 kept (Figure 5, right). On T0 the delivered walls are ° and ° from their cameras' mean views.
  4. Several walls. When each wall has two or more cameras, as on T0, each wall's cameras are calibrated on their own first, then the groups are joined by the rigid motion that maps their shared wand positions onto each other. The wand length fixes every group's scale. A camera alone behind its wall is placed against the others.

tube  The axis, then the distances

  1. The axis direction. Each linear camera magnifies more across the tube than along it, about 1.3× (Figure 3). The axis must be perpendicular to every camera's direction of higher magnification, which gives it directly. The world frame is then the tube's own: the axis is Y and Z points towards camera 1.
  2. The axis position, by search. Where the axis passes relative to the wand cloud is tried on a grid, every point that keeps the cloud inside the tube: positions mm apart, for each mirror image.
  3. Distances from the stretch. At each position, each camera is put on its line of sight at the distance where a model camera's stretch ratio (across over along) for the same cloud matches its linear camera's (Figure 6, right), and its focal length follows from the magnification along the axis.
  4. Rank by fitting. Every placement gets a quick coarse fit before it is ranked; the six best get a longer one. Ranking them as placed can mislead: with the orientations still about a degree off, a placement far from the tube can score best (seen in the tests).
Stage 3both

A coarse fit that is always defined

The final fit measures errors in pixels, which needs every ball's projection. From a rough start some projections do not exist: a ray misses the tube, or a ball lands on the wrong side of a wall. Stage 3 works the other way round. It traces each detection's ray into the water, triangulates each ball from its rays, sets the two balls exactly one wand length apart, and measures how far each ray misses its ball, scaled to pixels. That cost always exists, and a robust loss keeps a few bad detections from steering it.

Only the cameras (and the walls) are adjusted; the ball positions follow from them by triangulation each time, so they never lag behind.

Stage 4both

The final fit

The answer is the maximum-likelihood fit: every detection's error in pixels, squared and summed over all cameras and frames, minimised by Levenberg–Marquardt.

  • The unknowns. The rig: each camera's rotation, position and focal length (and k1), each wall's angle and position. T0 has of them; the tube rig . And every frame's wand: its midpoint and direction, 5 numbers, with the length held at L. T0 has of those.
  • Solved fast. A frame's 5 unknowns touch only that frame's detections, so they are eliminated frame by frame (the Schur complement). Each step solves only the rig's small system, and the cost grows only linearly with the number of frames.
  • The frame of reference. With flat walls nothing in the scene is fixed, so camera 1's position and orientation are held and the answer is turned to wall 1 afterwards. A tube is its own frame; only a spin about its axis and a slide along it are invisible, and camera 1 removes them.
  • Robust. Detections more than 5σ off are dropped, and come back if they fit later. Each camera's noise is estimated from its residuals and weights its detections.

Flat walls only. On rigs with several walls the fit restarts from a few answers perturbed by its own error bars and keeps a restart only if it explains the data better: this frees pinned fits. A wall seen only by one or two long-lens cameras gets its angle scanned, because its true angle can sit in a narrow valley far from a wrong answer. Optional held facts (constraints) can keep the wand inside a channel of known size or say where cameras aim, and fit_lens adds the principal point, k2 and tangential distortion. T0's delivered calibration holds the channel and the aim.

Tube only. Near the tube's wall a ball can have two images; balls whose second image lies within 30 px are dropped, since a detector could mix them up. When the coarse fit leaves more than 1 px, every short-listed start is carried through stage 4, because noisy data can favour a start with the cameras too far away. The tube cannot pin a fit: it does not move, and its model projects a ball wherever it is.

Stage 5both

Error bars and output

The fit's covariance gives every parameter's error bar and the error it predicts for points in 3-D. The output is a pyOpenLPT camera file per camera (PINPLATE or PINCYL), a result file with everything, and a text report that also warns about weak spots: for flat walls a weakly seen wall, a pinned fit, a hint far from the answer, too few frames.
